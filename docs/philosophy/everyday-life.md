# Everyday Life — Integration in Plain Language

> For readers who don't live in the codebase. The same idea as the rest of
> this folder, told with things you can touch — no jargon, no diagrams.
> If you've ever dropped a baton at a neighborhood event, you already
> understand most of it.

## The one idea

Everything worth using is made of *different parts doing different jobs*,
held together by *linkage* — the sequence, the timing, the agreements, the
practice that connects them. The parts are the cheap part. The linkage is
the rare part, and the part that decides whether the whole works.

There are two ways to get it wrong, and you've seen both:

- **Parts without linkage.** A pile of good ingredients that never get
  cooked into a dish. At the neighborhood, a "bag of features" nobody
  connects.
- **Linkage without real differentiation.** Everything mashed together so
  nothing gets its moment — or one part doing all the jobs alone. At the
  neighborhood, a kitchen where everyone stirs the same pot.

Three everyday versions first. Then the same shape at the neighborhood.

## Cooking a dish

**Differentiation:** Each ingredient has a role. Onion is aromatic base.
Garlic is pungent accent. Tomato is acidity and body. Olive oil is fat and
heat transfer. Salt is seasoning. They are *different things doing
different jobs.* You wouldn't substitute tomato for onion and expect the
dish to work.

**Linkage:** The method. Sautéing the onion first builds a flavor base
that the garlic then joins. Adding tomato later, after the aromatics are
softened, changes the chemistry. The *sequence and temperature* are the
linkage. They connect the parts into a flavor profile none of them
produces alone.

**What breaks:** Tossing everything into a pot at once (linkage without
real differentiation — you haven't given each ingredient its moment, so
nothing develops its role). Or making a "soup" where every ingredient is
just "a thing in the broth" (differentiation without linkage — no
sequence, no interaction, no transformation between them). It's a bag of
wet vegetables.

## A relay race

**Differentiation:** Four runners, four legs. The first runner is fast out
of the blocks; the last is the closer. The team is strong because each
runner is *good at their part*, not because everyone is average at all
four.

**Linkage:** The handoff. The baton only works if the outgoing runner
trusts the incoming one's speed, if they've practiced the exchange zone
together, if there's a signal — "take it now." Timing, trust, and
rehearsal are the linkage. Nothing about the baton is about running.

**What breaks:** A dropped baton. Four excellent runners, and the team
loses in the seam. Or the other way: a team of generalists where no one is
fastest on their leg — the parts are interchangeable, so nothing is good.

## An orchestra

**Differentiation:** Strings, woodwinds, brass, percussion — different
voices, different jobs. The oboe tunes the room; the cellos carry the
bass; the flutes carry the melody. You wouldn't swap the timpani for a
saxophone and expect the same piece.

**Linkage:** The score, the conductor, and — the part everyone
underestimates — *listening to each other*. The score is a contract: who
plays what, when, at what volume. The conductor is the live correction
when the contract meets reality. And the musicians adjust tempo and
balance against the people next to them, in real time.

**What breaks:** Every section playing their own piece at full volume —
parts without a shared score, which is just noise. Or one instrument
trying to play the whole orchestra's part alone — one part carrying a job
that needs many.

## The same shape at the neighborhood

Now the same table, at the neighborhood's scales:

|               | Parts (the cheap)         | Linkage (the rare)                | What the whole gets          |
|---------------|---------------------------|-----------------------------------|------------------------------|
| A shared event | people, supplies, a date | the coordination between them     | a neighborhood that shows up |
| A community project | contributors, materials | the linking of goals, owners, tasks | an outcome none of them could do alone |
| A group       | people with different skills | who-knows-whom, communication, delegation | a group that actually gets things done |
| The platform  | components, groups, features | the access model, the value chains | a shared place that holds    |

The everyday failure modes map straight across:

- **The dropped baton** — everyone's fine on their own, but the
  connection fails. Each person's having a great time, but the thing
  that needed someone to take it forward just stops — the "I'll handle
  that" nobody ever handles. At the neighborhood: the report that never
  reaches a moderator, the "I'll do it" that evaporates by morning.
- **The bag of wet vegetables** — a bunch of separate things that never
  become a whole. Every activity is fine on its own, but it never adds
  up to an evening, a project, a community you can point to. At the
  neighborhood: a pile of features that each work alone but don't add
  up to a place.
- **The solo instrument** — one person doing all the work. The friend
  who plans every get-together, remembers every detail, keeps everyone
  in touch. If they're not around, nothing happens. At the
  neighborhood: the one person everyone reaches out to, the one who
  holds everything together.
- **The unpracticed handoff** — the glue lives in one person's head.
  Only they know how the group works — who's good at what, who's quiet,
  how things get decided. When they're gone, the glue goes with them.
  At the neighborhood: the way things "just work" that only one person
  knows, or the group that only exists in someone's memory.

## Three questions to ask

Before a get-together, a project, a group of friends — three questions,
two minutes:

1. **Does it come back around?** If you start something — a question, a
   plan, a favor — can it finish *here*, with the people who started it?
   Or does it have to leave the room to get done — a text that needs to be
   forwarded, a "I'll ask someone else," a "let's circle back next week"?
   Every time it has to leave is a place where it'll probably just… stop.
2. **Where does it get passed by hand?** Who has to tell the next person?
   Who has to re-explain what's already been decided? Every "so I told
   Maria, and she told the others" is a place where something gets lost,
   or where someone has to do the same thing twice.
3. **Is one person carrying the whole thing?** Is the whole evening — or
   the whole project, or the whole group — only working because one person
   is doing all the thinking, all the remembering, all the holding
   together? If they leave the room, what's left?
