# Further Reading — The Traditions Behind Fractal Integration

> Optional. Nothing here is required to use these guidelines — the whole
> onboarding for Kumunita lives in this folder and takes about an hour
> (`START-HERE.md` is the whole onboarding). This is for when "why this
> matters" makes you want to go check the source, or when you want to hand a
> skeptical colleague something with more weight behind it.

As `START-HERE.md` says up front: none of this is new. It's a shared,
practical vocabulary for a pattern that several serious, independent
traditions have converged on from different directions — biology,
engineering, economics, neuroscience. Below is one solid entry point into
each, with a short note on where it shows up in this folder. Read the one
that matches whatever page sent you here; you don't need all of them.

## Systems theory — why "a system" is a real unit, not a metaphor

The claim that a whole made of interacting parts has properties none of the
parts have alone predates these guidelines by about seventy years. Ludwig
von Bertalanffy proposed treating "system" itself as the basic unit of
study, across biology, physics, and the social sciences — the direct
ancestor of principle 1 (quality is the quality of the linkage) and the
whole idea of applying one lens across every scale.

- Ludwig von Bertalanffy, *General System Theory: Foundations, Development,
  Applications* (1968) — [overview and context](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_System_Theory:_Foundations,_Development,_Applications) ·
  [full text at the Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/generalsystemthe0000bert_f7s2)

## Cybernetics — feedback, control, and why loops need an owner

Cybernetics is the study of how systems regulate themselves through
feedback — the direct ancestor of principle 4 (feedback loops are a
feature) and every "every loop needs an owner and a response" line in this
folder. Ashby's *Law of Requisite Variety* — a controller needs at least
as much variety as the thing it's controlling — is a precise, older version
of why under-differentiation (the god module, the one person everyone
depends on) fails.

- Norbert Wiener, *Cybernetics: Or Control and Communication in the Animal
  and the Machine* (1948) — [overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cybernetics:_Or_Control_and_Communication_in_the_Animal_and_the_Machine)
  (the book that named the field)
- W. Ross Ashby, *An Introduction to Cybernetics* (1956) — the more
  readable, more practical starting point.
  [Free full text](https://archive.org/details/AnIntroductionToCybernetics)
  (public domain)

## Systems thinking and leverage points — where to actually push

Donella Meadows took systems theory out of the lab and into practice:
where a system is stubborn (stocks, flows) and where a small push produces
a large, durable change (a feedback loop, a goal, a paradigm). Her essay is
the closest thing in this tradition to a "which arrow does this move?"
heuristic, applied to entire systems instead of one platform.

- Donella Meadows, *Thinking in Systems: A Primer* (2008) —
  [overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thinking_In_Systems:_A_Primer)
- Donella Meadows, *Leverage Points: Places to Intervene in a System*
  (1999) — [free full text](https://donellameadows.org/archives/leverage-points-places-to-intervene-in-a-system/)
  (short, and the single best next read if you only read one thing here)

## Institutional economics — how communities govern shared, differentiated resources

Elinor Ostrom's lifelong study of how real communities manage shared
resources (fisheries, irrigation systems, forests) without either top-down
control or collapse is one of the clearest large-scale cases for how this
works in practice, and one of the clearest cases for why it breaks down
when it doesn't: durable systems are the ones with living rules for
monitoring, feedback, and graduated response — not the ones with the most
rules on paper. It won her the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences,
the first awarded to a woman. Kumunita is a small-scale case of
exactly this kind of shared-resource problem: a platform the neighborhood
trusts to hold a shared place.

- Elinor Ostrom, *Governing the Commons: The Evolution of Institutions for
  Collective Action* (1990) —
  [an appreciation and summary](https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/lsereviewofbooks/2012/06/17/elinor-ostroms-work-on-governing-the-commons-an-appreciation/)

## Modularity — the engineering theory behind "differentiation is deliberate"

Baldwin and Clark's study of the computer industry is the most rigorous
account of why deliberate differentiation (splitting a system into modules
on purpose, with explicit interfaces) creates value, and why the *design
rules at the boundary* — not the modules themselves — are what makes
modularity pay off. It is the direct academic ancestor of
`in-code.md`'s "boundaries are contracts" and "public surface is permanent
integration cost."

- Carliss Y. Baldwin & Kim B. Clark, *Design Rules, Vol. 1: The Power of
  Modularity* (2000) — [publisher page](https://mitpress.mit.edu/books/design-rules-volume-1) ·
  [a 20-years-later retrospective, more accessible than the original](https://academic.oup.com/icc/article/32/1/1/6972656)

## Interpersonal Neurobiology — integration as the definition of a healthy mind

`domains-of-integration.md` is a direct, explicit re-mapping of Daniel
Siegel's work: he names nine domains in which the mind links differentiated
aspects of experience into a coherent, healthy whole, and argues (with the
FACES acronym this folder borrows outright) that dysfunction is almost
always a failure of one specific kind of linkage, not a missing part. This
is the most direct source in this folder — read this one if you want to see
where `domains-of-integration.md` actually came from.

- Daniel J. Siegel, *Mindsight: The New Science of Personal Transformation*
  (2010) — [Dr. Siegel's own page on Interpersonal Neurobiology](https://drdansiegel.com/interpersonal-neurobiology/)
- [A shorter framework overview](https://www.psychalive.org/framework-cultivating-integration/)

## Integrated Information Theory — consciousness as integration, taken to its limit

Giulio Tononi's Integrated Information Theory (IIT) proposes that
consciousness *just is* integrated information — a system's irreducibility
to its parts, made mathematically precise. It's the most literal version of
this claim, pushed all the way to "this is what experience is," and it's
genuinely contested: it's one serious theory among several competing ones.
Read it as the strongest, most rigorous version of the idea — and as a
useful example of a claim this folder deliberately does *not* make (we say
integration is a useful lens for building things; IIT says it's the
literal substance of awareness).

- [Scholarpedia overview, written by Tononi](http://www.scholarpedia.org/article/Integrated_information_theory)
[Integrated Information Theory of Consciousness — Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/integrated-information-theory-of-consciousness/)

## Conway — the org-chart version of the same claim

Conway's Law — "organizations which design systems … are constrained to
produce designs which are copies of the organization's communication
structures" — is probably the most widely-known restatement of the same
pattern, in the direction from org to system. The reverse, that systems
shape the org, is less widely known but equally real. Read the original.

- Melvin E. Conway, *How Do Committees Invent?* (1968) —
  [the original article, still hosted by Conway himself](https://www.melconway.com/law/index.html)

the way you'd add to any other part of this folder, and note the addition
in `SOURCE.md` so it's visible next time we re-sync.
